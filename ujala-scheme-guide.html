UJALA Scheme

Unnat Jyoti by Affordable LEDs for All – Promoting Energy-Efficient Lighting Across India

1. What is the UJALA Scheme?

UJALA stands for Unnat Jyoti by Affordable LEDs for All. It is a Government of India initiative launched to make energy-efficient LED lighting affordable and accessible to domestic consumers.

The programme encouraged households to replace traditional incandescent bulbs with LED bulbs. By purchasing LEDs in bulk and distributing them at affordable prices, the programme helped reduce the initial cost of energy-efficient lighting.

Full Form: Unnat Jyoti by Affordable LEDs for All (UJALA).

2. Launch Date and Ministry

  • Launch date: 5 January 2015.
  • Launched by: Prime Minister Narendra Modi.
  • Ministry: Ministry of Power.
  • Implementing agency: Energy Efficiency Services Limited (EESL).
  • Earlier name: Domestic Efficient Lighting Programme (DELP).
  • Programme type: National energy-efficiency initiative.

3. Main Objectives of UJALA

The main objective was to encourage the use of energy-efficient lighting and reduce household electricity consumption.

  • Make LED bulbs affordable for ordinary households.
  • Reduce electricity consumption through efficient lighting.
  • Reduce household electricity bills.
  • Replace inefficient incandescent bulbs with LED technology.
  • Create awareness about energy conservation.
  • Reduce greenhouse gas emissions associated with electricity generation.
  • Encourage manufacturers to produce energy-efficient lighting products.
  • Transform India's domestic lighting market.

4. What Products Were Offered?

UJALA initially focused on LED bulbs and later included other energy-efficient electrical appliances.

  • LED bulbs: Energy-efficient alternatives to traditional incandescent bulbs.
  • LED tube lights: Efficient lighting products for homes and other spaces.
  • Energy-efficient fans: Fans designed to consume less electricity than conventional models.
LED stands for Light Emitting Diode. LED technology produces light using semiconductor components and is generally more energy-efficient than traditional incandescent lighting.

5. Who Could Benefit?

UJALA was designed primarily for domestic electricity consumers across India.

  • Households with a metered electricity connection.
  • Consumers served by electricity distribution companies (DISCOMs).
  • Rural households.
  • Urban households.
  • Consumers interested in reducing electricity consumption.
Under the original distribution arrangements, domestic households with a metered connection from their electricity distribution company were eligible to purchase UJALA products.

6. How Did UJALA Make LEDs Affordable?

One of the biggest barriers to LED adoption was the high initial purchase price. UJALA addressed this problem through demand aggregation and bulk procurement.

  • EESL estimated the demand for LED products across the country.
  • Large quantities of products were procured through competitive bidding.
  • Bulk procurement helped reduce the purchase cost.
  • LED products were distributed to consumers at affordable prices.
  • Increased adoption helped expand the domestic LED market.
The programme helped transform LED lighting from an expensive product into a widely accessible household lighting option.

7. Prices of Products Under UJALA

According to the Government's January 2025 UJALA anniversary report, the programme's reported distribution prices included:

  • LED bulb: Approximately ₹70.
  • LED tube light: Approximately ₹220.
  • Energy-efficient fan: Approximately ₹1,110.

These prices were determined through competitive procurement and included costs associated with distribution, maintenance, awareness and administration.

These are reported programme prices, not guaranteed current retail prices. Availability and prices may differ across sellers and locations.

8. How Much Electricity Can LED Bulbs Save?

LED bulbs generally require less electricity than incandescent bulbs to produce a similar amount of light.

  • A 7W LED bulb can provide illumination comparable to a 60W incandescent bulb.
  • LEDs can use substantially less electricity than traditional incandescent bulbs.
  • Lower electricity consumption can reduce household electricity bills.
  • LEDs generally have a longer operating life than traditional incandescent bulbs.
Example: A 7W LED bulb uses approximately 7 units of power for every 1,000 hours of operation. A 60W incandescent bulb uses approximately 60 units for the same duration.

9. Documents Required During Distribution

Under the original UJALA distribution arrangements, consumers were asked to provide documents depending on their payment method.

  • For upfront payment: Government-authorised identity proof.
  • For EMI payment: Copy of the latest electricity bill and government-authorised identity proof.

Consumers also needed to have a metered domestic electricity connection from their respective DISCOM.

10. How Could Consumers Purchase UJALA Products?

During the programme's active distribution period, EESL and participating electricity distribution companies arranged special distribution counters and kiosks.

  • Consumers could check the designated distribution counters in their city.
  • They could visit the authorised counter with the required documents.
  • They could choose an available payment option.
  • Products could be purchased through upfront payment or eligible instalment arrangements.
UJALA's original nationwide distribution programme is no longer in active implementation. The historical kiosk process should not be treated as a current application procedure.

11. Role of EESL

Energy Efficiency Services Limited (EESL) was the main implementing agency for UJALA.

  • Aggregated demand for LED products.
  • Procured products through competitive bidding.
  • Worked with electricity distribution companies.
  • Organised product distribution.
  • Helped increase consumer awareness about energy efficiency.
  • Supported the development of the energy-efficient lighting market.

EESL is a joint venture of public-sector energy companies under the Ministry of Power.

12. Achievements of the UJALA Scheme

UJALA became one of the world's largest domestic energy-efficient lighting programmes.

  • LED bulbs distributed: Approximately 36.87 crore.
  • LED tube lights distributed: Approximately 72.2 lakh.
  • Energy-efficient fans distributed: Approximately 23.6 lakh.
  • Annual energy savings: Approximately 48.4 billion kWh.
  • Annual monetary savings: Approximately ₹19,153 crore, as reported in the January 2025 government update.
  • Annual CO₂ emission reduction: Approximately 39 million tonnes.
These are government-reported programme figures. Energy and monetary savings are estimates based on programme assumptions and reported deployment.

13. Environmental Benefits

The widespread adoption of LED technology supports environmental protection by reducing electricity consumption.

  • Lower electricity demand from lighting.
  • Reduced greenhouse gas emissions associated with power generation.
  • More efficient use of energy resources.
  • Reduced need for frequent replacement because LEDs generally last longer.
  • Greater awareness of sustainable consumption.

14. Difference Between LED and Traditional Bulbs

  • Electricity consumption: LEDs generally use much less electricity for comparable brightness.
  • Operating life: LEDs generally last longer than incandescent bulbs.
  • Heat generation: Incandescent bulbs waste a large proportion of energy as heat.
  • Efficiency: LEDs convert a greater share of electrical energy into visible light.
  • Maintenance: Longer operating life can reduce replacement frequency.

15. Current Status of UJALA

UJALA achieved its intended objectives and reached saturation in 2019. According to a parliamentary response, EESL is no longer implementing the UJALA programme as an active distribution scheme.

  • Launch: 5 January 2015.
  • Achievement of original objectives: 2019.
  • Reported LED bulb distribution: 36.87 crore.
  • Active nationwide implementation: No longer active.
  • Product availability: EESL continues to sell LED bulbs through its online platform and institutional channels, according to the parliamentary response.
UJALA should not be advertised as a currently open government scheme offering free LED bulbs or a new household subsidy application.

16. UJALA vs Street Lighting National Programme (SLNP)

Both initiatives were launched on 5 January 2015, but they focused on different types of lighting.

  • UJALA: Focused on energy-efficient lighting products for domestic consumers.
  • SLNP: Focused on replacing conventional streetlights with energy-efficient LED streetlights.
  • UJALA implementing agency: EESL, working with distribution partners.
  • SLNP implementing agency: EESL, working with urban local bodies, municipalities and Gram Panchayats.

17. Important Points to Remember

  • UJALA stands for Unnat Jyoti by Affordable LEDs for All.
  • It was launched on 5 January 2015.
  • It was earlier known as the Domestic Efficient Lighting Programme (DELP).
  • The Ministry of Power is the responsible ministry.
  • EESL was the implementing agency.
  • The programme promoted affordable LED bulbs, tube lights and energy-efficient fans.
  • Bulk procurement helped reduce product prices.
  • Approximately 36.87 crore LED bulbs were distributed.
  • The scheme achieved its original objectives and is no longer actively implemented by EESL.

19. Conclusion

The UJALA Scheme played an important role in transforming India's domestic lighting market. By making LED products affordable through bulk procurement and large-scale distribution, it encouraged millions of households to adopt energy-efficient lighting.

Its achievements include reduced electricity consumption, lower household lighting costs and reduced emissions. Although the original programme is no longer actively implemented, its impact continues to be visible in India's widespread adoption of LED lighting.
